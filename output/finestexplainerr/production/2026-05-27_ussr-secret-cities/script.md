Arzamas-16. For forty years it did not appear on any map. Not in encyclopedias, not in atlases, not in any official Soviet document. Behind kilometers of barbed wire, sand strips raked every morning for footprints, and armed patrols, 80,000 people lived and worked on the bombs that could end the world. Above ground: forests and ordinary Soviet life. Below the fence: an entire city whose name was a state secret even from most of its own citizens.

Arzamas-16, later known as Sarov, was the Soviet Union's primary nuclear weapons design center. Founded in 1946 on the grounds of a former monastery, it housed the All-Russian Scientific Research Institute of Experimental Physics, or VNIIEF. This was the Soviet Los Alamos. The first Soviet atomic bomb, RDS-1, tested in 1949, was designed here. Subsequent thermonuclear weapons followed. Construction relied heavily on prisoner labor. Residents were officially registered at a single Moscow address so their real location would remain hidden. Privileged housing, better food, higher pay, and near-zero crime came with total isolation: mail censored, travel required permits, and the outside world did not know the city existed.

The work never really stopped. Today Sarov remains one of Russia's two main nuclear design centers under Rosatom. It is still a closed city. Special permits are required even for Russian citizens. Foreigners are almost never allowed inside. A place built in total secrecy for the most destructive weapons on Earth continues to exist behind the same fences.

Pripyat. In 1986, 49,400 people lived here. The average age was 26. It was a model Soviet atomgrad, built in the early 1970s to house workers and families from the nearby Chernobyl Nuclear Power Plant. Wide boulevards, new apartment blocks, schools, a hospital, a palace of culture, and an amusement park scheduled to open on 1 May.

On 26 April 1986, Reactor 4 exploded. For 36 hours the city was told nothing. Then loudspeakers announced a temporary evacuation. Residents were instructed to pack documents, warm clothes, and enough food for three days. Over 1,000 buses brought from Kyiv removed nearly the entire population in a few hours. Most never returned. The city that had not existed on some maps before the disaster now truly disappeared from everyday life.

Pripyat remains a ghost town inside the 30-kilometer Chernobyl Exclusion Zone. Trees grow through the asphalt. Gas masks and textbooks lie where they were dropped. The Ferris wheel in the amusement park never turned for the public. Radiation levels vary, and the zone is still restricted, especially since 2022. A city of almost 50,000 people became zero overnight, and the absence is permanent.

Ozersk. In the late 1940s, more than 40,000 Gulag prisoners and prisoners of war built a secret plutonium production complex and the city around it in the southern Urals. On paper it was Chelyabinsk-40, later Chelyabinsk-65. On no map it was Ozersk. The Mayak Production Association began operations in 1948 and produced the plutonium for the first Soviet nuclear test.

Haste and secrecy produced disaster. Between 1949 and 1956, large quantities of radioactive waste were dumped directly into the Techa River. Lake Karachay was used as an open-air waste repository. Then, on 29 September 1957, a cooling system failure in an underground waste tank led to a chemical explosion. Approximately 20 million curies of radioactive material were released. The East Ural Radioactive Trace contaminated between 15,000 and 23,000 square kilometers. Ten thousand people were eventually evacuated from the worst-affected villages, some of them years later. The Soviet government covered up the accident for more than thirty years. The disaster became known to the outside world only as the Kyshtym accident, named after the nearest open town.

Ozersk is still a closed city today. Mayak continues to operate, reprocessing spent fuel and producing isotopes. The region remains one of the most radioactively contaminated places on Earth from the cumulative legacy of early Soviet nuclear production. A city built in total secrecy also hid one of the worst nuclear accidents in history until long after the Soviet Union itself had collapsed.

Mezhgorye. In the late 1970s, the Soviet Union began carving a massive underground complex beneath Mount Yamantau, the highest peak in the southern Urals. The associated closed city, known by codes such as Beloretsk-16, received official town status only in 1995 and is now called Mezhgorye. Its population is roughly 16,000. The mountain above it is said to contain command facilities protected by hundreds of meters of quartz-rich rock, chosen for its resistance to electromagnetic pulse and nuclear blast effects.

After the Soviet collapse, construction reportedly continued into the 2000s despite economic hardship. Official explanations have included mining operations, strategic food reserves, or a repository for national treasures. Western intelligence assessments have consistently described it as a hardened strategic command and control center intended for national leadership continuity in a major conflict, comparable in concept to American sites such as Raven Rock or Cheyenne Mountain. The area maintains a no-fly zone and is under direct federal control rather than regional authority. Open-source satellite analysis as recently as 2025-2026 shows ongoing maintenance and military unit activity in parts of the complex.

Mezhgorye remains one of the most restricted locations in Russia. Entry requires federal permits. The mountain complex is believed to be at least partially operational for strategic purposes. While some Cold War bunkers in the West eventually became known or even visited, this one continues to exist in near-total secrecy decades after the empire that ordered its construction ceased to exist.

Kurchatov. In 1947, Lavrentiy Beria selected a remote stretch of steppe in what is now Kazakhstan for the Soviet Union's primary nuclear weapons test site. The closed support city built beside it was known as Semipalatinsk-21, or by the railway station name Konechnaya, "the end of the line." Its official name today is Kurchatov. Between 1949 and 1989, 456 nuclear devices were detonated at the Semipalatinsk Test Site, 116 of them atmospheric or surface tests. The first Soviet atomic bomb, RDS-1, was detonated on 29 August 1949. The mushroom cloud was visible from the town.

The site covered roughly 18,000 square kilometers. Scientists and military personnel lived in the closed city with privileged supplies while fallout drifted over downwind villages whose residents had no warning and no protection. An estimated one to one and a half million people were exposed to radioactive contamination over four decades. The last test occurred in 1989. On 29 August 1991, exactly 42 years after the first detonation, Kazakhstan closed the site by presidential decree. The newly independent country later renounced the nuclear arsenal it had inherited, at the time the fourth largest in the world.

The town of Kurchatov saw its population collapse from as many as 50,000 to around 8,000 in the immediate aftermath. Today it hosts Kazakhstan's National Nuclear Center, which conducts research and remediation work. Parts of the former test site have been opened for limited tourism and economic use, but many zones remain contaminated or restricted. The city built to test the end of the world became, for a time, a ghost of the empire that created it.

Zheleznogorsk. In 1950, deep in the Siberian taiga north of Krasnoyarsk, the Soviet Union began construction of another secret plutonium production complex. The city was known only as Krasnoyarsk-26. Up to 70,000 Gulag prisoners excavated a granite mountain to house the Mining and Chemical Combine at depths of 200 to 250 meters. Three graphite-moderated reactors were installed inside the rock. The facility ultimately produced an estimated 40 to 45 tons of weapons-grade plutonium, roughly one-third of the entire Soviet stockpile.

The underground location was chosen for protection against aerial reconnaissance and attack. Reactors also supplied heat and electricity to the city above. After the Cold War, two reactors were shut down in the early 1990s. The third continued operating until 2010, primarily to provide district heating, and marked the end of weapons-grade plutonium production in Russia. The United States provided hundreds of millions of dollars in assistance to help secure materials and convert parts of the complex to civilian work.

Zheleznogorsk was officially renamed only in the 1990s and remains a closed city. Population today is approximately 82,000 to 85,000. The mountain complex, once the most secret part of the Soviet nuclear archipelago, now supports spent fuel reprocessing and advanced reactor fuel programs under Rosatom. The bear tearing apart an atom on the old city coat of arms still feels like an accurate symbol: a place built in total secrecy that helped arm one superpower and then quietly adapted when that superpower disappeared.

The Soviet Union dissolved in 1991. The ideology, the maps, and most of the official stories disappeared with it. But the closed cities, the barbed wire, the underground command posts, and the radioactive traces did not. Some of these places are still working on weapons or strategic systems. Others are still dangerous to approach. All of them were constructed so that the outside world would never have to know they existed.

Above ground, ordinary life continues in Russia and Kazakhstan. Behind the fences, inside the mountains, and across the exclusion zones, another country persists. One the maps were never meant to show, and in many cases still do not.
